Which content pages should an editor review first?

A leakage-checked ranking built on about 313,000 real pages of search data

Mohamed Khaled · October 2026

AbstractIntroductionDataMethodologyResultsLimitationsRecommendationsReproducibilityAcknowledgments

Abstract

Content teams have far more pages than review hours, so choosing which pages to look at first has a real cost. Using search-performance data from the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse (about 313,000 pages across 50 clients, March 2026), I asked which pages lose clicks in the second half of a month, given only first-half signals. I compared a hand-written rule with a Random Forest, validating on held-out clients rather than random rows and auditing every feature for leakage. On held-out clients the model reached an AUC of 0.862 against a base rate of about 10%; an earlier version scored 0.956 until two label-adjacent features and one invalid date field were removed. The result is a directional, decision-support ranking meant for human editors: it covers one month and search data only, and it makes no causal claim.

1. Introduction: a decision with a price tag

Picture a content editor at a company that publishes thousands of web pages. This week there are a few hours of review time. Every hour spent on a page that was never going to move is an hour not spent on a page that was quietly losing visitors. The decision is easy to state and expensive to get wrong: which pages get looked at first?

That is the real problem behind this study. FlyRank builds and monitors large volumes of published content for its clients, and its workflow already flags pages for refresh or click-through fixes using hand-written rules. FlyRank’s own March 2026 report, The State of AI-Driven SEO in Numbers, argues for simple, validated comparisons and for refreshing strong content before it decays. This note takes one lane of that problem, prioritising pages for review, and asks a narrower question: can a model that sees only what was known by mid-month rank pages by how likely their clicks are to fall during the rest of the month, and how much of its apparent skill survives honest validation?

The answer is partly encouraging and partly a lesson in caution. A first model scored an AUC of 0.956, a figure inflated by features tied to how the label was built. After repairs and a split by client, the measured figure was 0.862. The sections below show how that number was earned, where it stops being valid, and how a person should use the resulting queue.

What was excluded, and why

  • GA4 engagement columns. Only 4.2% of March rows had GA4 data available, too sparse to use. The model therefore sees search visibility only.
  • Deleted pages. No editor action is possible on them.
  • AI-referrer breakdowns. Real data, but outside the scope of a review-priority lane.
  • Days since last update. The page table stores only the latest update date, so for 88.1% of pages that date falls after the 15 March cut-off and cannot describe what was known at the time. The feature was dropped rather than patched.
  • Identifiers. Client and page IDs are pseudonymous hashes. They were used for joining and grouping only, never as features, and none appear in this note.

3. Methodology

Unit and label

One row is one page in March 2026. A page is labelled declining when its clicks in 16–31 March are lower than its clicks in 1–15 March. Because the label is built from second-half data, nothing from that period may appear in a feature.

Features

Eleven inputs, all known by 15 March or fixed attributes of the page: first-half impressions, first-half average position, page age, word count, search volume, backlinks, category count, a flag for whether keyword data exist, content type, search intent, and competition level.

Baseline rule

The comparison is a hand-written rule in the spirit of a refresh flag: a page scores if it has not been updated for 91 or more days, still receives impressions, and has a click-through rate below the average for its position band; the score is its impressions. Two signal checks came first. Click-through rate falls steadily as position worsens (confirmed; unweighted mean per page: 0.93% for positions up to 3, 0.47% for 3–10, 0.33% for 10–20, and 0.21% beyond 20). Staleness behaved the opposite way to the rule’s premise: among pages with a valid update date, 17.2% of those updated within 30 days declined versus 0.1% of those untouched for 181 days or more. That check covers only the roughly 12% of pages whose recorded update date precedes the cut-off.

Models and validation

Logistic Regression (standardised inputs) and a Random Forest (300 trees, depth 8, seed 42). Pages were split by client: 40 clients (270,159 pages) for training and 10 clients (43,111 pages) for testing, with zero clients in both. Scores are reported as precision at K (what an editor working down a queue experiences) and AUC, always next to the base rate, which is 10.6% in the test split.

Leakage checks

  • Timeline: the last feature date (15 March) precedes the first label date (16 March).
  • Label-adjacent features: first-half clicks are one side of the inequality that defines the label, and first-half click-through rate is derived from them. Both were removed.
  • Invalid date field: days since update, described above, was removed.
  • Split design: every model was also scored under a random row split to measure how much that design flatters results.
  • Importance method: permutation importance was scored on AUC, because the default (accuracy) showed zero importance for every feature at a 10% base rate.
  • Product flags: none exist in these tables, so no existing system score could leak in.

On the held-out clients, the Random Forest ranked pages better than the baseline rule at the top of the queue and overall.

Precision at small K is noisy: a re-run of the same Random Forest on the same split gave 0.40, 0.45 and 0.36 at K = 10, 20 and 50, while AUC was identical (0.862). Read top-of-queue figures as roughly ±0.1.

What the model leans on

First-half impressions dominate, followed by average position. Impressions are correlated with the first-half clicks that were removed, so part of that importance may be a diluted echo of how the label is built; this was not separately tested (see Limitations).

Where it is wrong

At a 0.5 cut-off the error rate was 11.2% for keyword articles, 4.7% for comparison articles and 0.7% for the smallest content type. By position band it was highest for pages in the top 3 (23.2%), similar for positions 3–10 and 10–20 (20.9% and 21.4%), and lowest beyond position 20 (13.0%), where little room remains to fall. The misses inspected were low-visibility, deep-position pages (for example 33 and 11 first-half impressions at average positions near 33 and 54): too little signal to say anything confident. The model’s scores are compressed, with the highest in the whole panel near 0.53, so it works as a ranking, not as a calibrated probability.

5. Limitations and honest framing

  • One month, one portfolio. Validated on March 2026 and 10 held-out clients. It has not been checked on another month or a brand-new client.
  • Search data only. With GA4 at 4.2% coverage, the model says nothing about on-page engagement or content quality.
  • The label is a raw comparison of two counts. That carries floor and regression-to-the-mean effects, and the second window (16 days) is one day longer than the first (15 days), which tilts the label slightly towards “not declining”. “Clicks fell” is not the same as “this page needs a refresh”.
  • Possible residual leak. First-half impressions may still carry part of the label’s construction. Not tested by ablation.
  • Update dates are a current snapshot. Staleness could not be modelled, and the baseline’s staleness rule and signal check cover only about 12% of pages.
  • Noisy at the top. Precision at 10 moved between runs by about 0.1.
  • Observational. Nothing here tests whether refreshing a page improves its clicks. Results are directional and decision-support only.
  • Selected population. Pages needed activity in both half-months; deleted pages were excluded.

Human review before acting

  • Read the actual page: is it still accurate, relevant and on-brand? The model never sees content quality.
  • Check the page is not already scheduled for removal, merging or a redesign.
  • Look for context the numbers cannot show: seasonal, legal or campaign-linked pages.
  • Spot-check rows from outside the top of the queue now and then, so a list that is only ever read at the top cannot drift unnoticed.

What should not be automated

  • Publishing or editing content from this queue. It says where to look, never what to write.
  • Deleting or de-indexing pages because they landed in no_action.
  • Applying the queue to a client or month it was not validated on, without repeating the split and leakage checks.

Cost, value and monitoring

The cost of a wrong pick is editor time. In the held-out test, roughly 0.45 to 0.50 of the top 20 pages were ones whose clicks fell, against a base rate near 0.11; that is a prioritisation aid, not evidence that a refresh will help. A click-value tiebreaker (clicks × cost per click) is a sensible next step but was not computed here. Re-run the evaluation monthly and consider retraining if top-of-queue precision falls toward the baseline, if the base rate drifts far from about 10%, if GA4 coverage rises enough to change the feature set, or if the page or client mix shifts materially.

7. Reproducibility

Everything is in the public repository: https://github.com/mohamedkhaled600/flyrank-internship-ml. Seeds are fixed at 42. To rerun, request access to the gated dataset on Hugging Face, store a read token as a Colab secret named HF_TOKEN, open a notebook in Colab and choose Run all. The ranked-queue CSV is regenerated on each run and is not stored in the repository; the run’s receipts are in work/outputs/w07_playbook_metrics.json.
